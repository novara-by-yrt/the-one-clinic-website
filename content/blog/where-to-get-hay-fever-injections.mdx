A hay fever injection is one of those options. It is not a cure, and it is not right for everyone. This guide explains what the injection is, how it works, who it suits, and where to get it safely.

<h2>What Are Hay Fever Injections?</h2>
A hay fever injection is a dose of corticosteroid, a type of steroid, given to calm a severe allergic reaction to pollen. Most UK clinics use a medicine called Kenalog, which contains triamcinolone. People often call it a hay fever jab.

The injection is not a vaccine. A vaccine trains your body to fight an infection. A hay fever steroid injection does the opposite. It gently lowers your immune system's overreaction so it stops attacking harmless pollen. It reduces the swelling and irritation that cause your symptoms.

A doctor gives the injection into the muscle of your upper buttock. It takes only a few minutes. One dose can ease symptoms for several weeks, and for many people it covers a large part of the pollen season.

It helps to be clear about what the injection does and does not do:
<ul>
 	<li>It reduces how severe your symptoms feel.</li>
 	<li>It works for several weeks, not forever.</li>
 	<li>It does not cure hay fever or stop it from returning next year.</li>
</ul>
A Kenalog injection for hay fever is a bigger step than everyday allergy medicine. The same kind of steroid also appears in other treatments, such as joint injections used to ease pain and swelling.
<h2>Symptoms of Severe Hay Fever</h2>
Mild hay fever is annoying. Severe hay fever can take over your day. The symptoms are not just a runny nose. They can affect your sleep, your focus, and your mood.

Common symptoms include:
<ul>
 	<li>Sneezing again and again</li>
 	<li>A blocked or runny nose</li>
 	<li>Itchy, red, or watery eyes</li>
 	<li>An itchy throat, mouth, or ears</li>
 	<li>Mucus running down the back of the throat</li>
 	<li>A cough caused by that mucus</li>
 	<li>Tiredness and broken sleep</li>
 	<li>Trouble concentrating</li>
</ul>
People with asthma often notice that their chests feel tighter during pollen season. If your symptoms stop you from sleeping or working for weeks at a time, it makes sense to ask a doctor about stronger options.
<h2>How Hay Fever Injections Work</h2>
A hay fever injection works by calming the immune system for several weeks. Once the steroid is in your body, it lowers the inflammation behind your symptoms. Many people feel some relief within one to two days, and the full effect usually settles in within about ten days.

A typical appointment follows four simple steps:
<ul>
 	<li><strong>Consultation:</strong> You meet a GMC-registered doctor face-to-face. It takes around 20 to 30 minutes.</li>
 	<li><strong>Health check:</strong> The doctor reviews your medical history and looks for any reason the injection might not be safe for you.</li>
 	<li><strong>The injection:</strong> If you are suitable, the doctor will inject it into the muscle in your upper buttock. This part takes only a few minutes.</li>
 	<li><strong>Aftercare:</strong> You can return to your normal day straight away. Mild soreness where the needle went in is normal and soon fades.</li>
</ul>
Most people need only one injection per pollen season. In extreme situations, doctors recommend taking another injection, but not earlier than two weeks after the first one.

<h2>Are Hay Fever Injections Safe?</h2>
A steroid injection is powerful, so it is not a first choice. The NHS stopped giving hay fever injections several years ago. Its guidelines decided that, for most people, the risks of a strong steroid outweigh the benefits when safer options exist.

That is why a good clinic treats the injection as a last step. A doctor will usually expect you to have already tried:
<ul>
 	<li>Non-drowsy antihistamine tablets</li>
 	<li>Steroid nasal sprays</li>
 	<li>Antihistamine eye drops</li>
</ul>
Because the steroid affects your whole body, it can cause side effects. It could include pain at the injection site, difficulty sleeping, changes in mood, increased appetite, and elevated blood glucose levels.

The injection does not suit everyone. A doctor may advise against it if you:
<ul>
 	<li>Have an infection</li>
 	<li>Have diabetes that is hard to control</li>
 	<li>Are pregnant or breastfeeding</li>
 	<li>Take medicines that lower your immune system</li>
</ul>
It is exactly why the consultation matters. A face-to-face check lets the doctor weigh up the benefits and risks for you as an individual.
