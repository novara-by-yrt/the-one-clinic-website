The hay fever injection can reduce severe hay fever symptoms for a few weeks, and some people with very bad symptoms do feel better after it. But the honest answer is more complex. The NHS stopped offering this injection in 2018 over safety concerns; it is not licensed to treat hay fever in the UK, and leading allergy experts no longer recommend it.

This guide explains how it works, how well it works, what the risks are, and the safer options most people should try first. If you want a full breakdown of the risks involved, our guide on <a href="/blog/side-effects-of-hayfever-injection">hay fever jab side effects</a> covers them in detail.
<h2>Key Takeaways</h2>
<ul>
 	<li>The hay fever injection is a strong steroid given into a muscle, not a quick "vaccine" against pollen.</li>
 	<li>It can ease severe symptoms for a short time, but the relief is not guaranteed and does not work for everyone.</li>
 	<li>The NHS withdrew it in 2018, and it is not licensed for hay fever in the UK.</li>
 	<li>It carries whole-body side effects because it cannot be removed once injected.</li>
 	<li>For most people, antihistamines, steroid nasal sprays, and eye drops control symptoms safely.</li>
 	<li>A doctor should always assess your symptoms and health before suggesting any prescription treatment.</li>
</ul>
<h2>How Does the Hay Fever Injection Work?</h2>
The injection works like a slow-release store of steroid medicine:
<ul>
 	<li>A doctor injects the steroid deep into a muscle.</li>
 	<li>The muscle holds the medicine and releases it slowly into the body.</li>
 	<li>The steroid lowers inflammation and the immune reaction to pollen.</li>
 	<li>Symptoms often ease over the following days as the medicine builds up.</li>
</ul>
Most people who respond notice a change within 24 to 72 hours. Because the steroid spreads throughout the body, it can affect far more than just your nose and eyes, which is why doctors are cautious about it.

<h2>How Well Does the Hay Fever Injection Work?</h2>
For people with severe hay fever, the injection can give real relief, and some say it is the only thing that has helped them. So it is fair to say it works for some.

But there are significant caveats:
<ul>
 	<li>The relief is short-term, and the dose cannot be adjusted once it is in.</li>
 	<li>The effect varies from person to person, and even from year to year in the same person.</li>
 	<li>The NHS judged that the risks outweigh the benefits for a seasonal allergy, and withdrew it.</li>
 	<li>Allergy UK does not recommend it for hay fever, in line with current UK guidance.</li>
</ul>
In short, it can reduce symptoms, but its effectiveness is not the full story. A treatment that works yet carries serious risks is not always the right choice, especially when safer options exist.
<h2>Hay Fever Injection Side Effects</h2>
Because it is a strong, whole-body steroid, the hay fever injection side effects can reach well beyond the injection site. Mild and short-lived effects include:
<ul>
 	<li>Soreness, swelling, or redness where the needle went in</li>
 	<li>Temporary facial flushing</li>
 	<li>Headache</li>
 	<li>Trouble sleeping</li>
</ul>
The steroid also affects the rest of the body, and this is the bigger concern. Possible effects include:
<ul>
 	<li>A weaker immune system, raising the risk of infections such as flu, chickenpox, and shingles</li>
 	<li>Mood changes, low mood, or irritability</li>
 	<li>Raised blood pressure</li>
 	<li>Raised blood sugar</li>
 	<li>Fluid retention and weight gain</li>
 	<li>Thinning of the bones over time, especially with repeated yearly use</li>
</ul>
A key point that sets this injection apart from tablets or spray is that once it is in the muscle, it cannot be removed. If you react badly, you and your doctor must wait for it to wear off.
<h2>Is the Hay Fever Injection Safe?</h2>
This is the heart of the matter. Whether the hay fever injection is safe depends on the person, but UK guidance is clear that the risks are significant.

Unlike a steroid nasal spray, which delivers a tiny dose straight to the nose, this injection floods the whole body with steroid. That is why allergy experts and the NHS treat it with caution.

The injection is not suitable for everyone. A doctor will not normally give it to:
<ul>
 	<li>Children</li>
 	<li>Women who are pregnant or breastfeeding</li>
 	<li>People with certain infections, diabetes, high blood pressure, or eye conditions such as glaucoma</li>
</ul>
It is also a prescription-only medicine, so a doctor must assess you in person before any treatment is given. You should never buy it online or from a salon, as the product may be fake, unsafe, or wrongly stored.

<h2>How Long Does the Hay Fever Injection Last?</h2>
Most people ask how long the hay fever injection lasts before booking. The honest answer is that it varies.
<ul>
 	<li>The medicine usually releases over about 3 to 8 weeks.</li>
 	<li>For some people, useful relief stretches across much of the pollen season.</li>
 	<li>The exact length depends on the dose, your body, and the pollen count that year.</li>
</ul>
This is one reason the effect is hard to predict. Tablets and sprays let you adjust treatment day-to-day. A single injection does not.

The cost of the hay fever injection in the UK is not fixed because it is a private treatment rather than an NHS one. Prices differ between clinics, and many UK clinics have listed it from around £75 to £150.

<ul>

<h2>Safer Alternatives to the Hay Fever Injection</h2>
For the vast majority of people, other treatments control hay fever well, with far fewer risks. A doctor or pharmacist can help you build the right mix.
<ul>
 	<li><strong>Antihistamine tablets:</strong> Modern non-drowsy types ease sneezing, itching, and a runny nose. They work best when you start them before the season.</li>
 	<li><strong>Steroid nasal sprays:</strong> These deliver a small, targeted dose to the nose and are very effective for congestion. They are a mainstay of hay fever care.</li>
 	<li><strong>Antihistamine eye drops:</strong> These calm itchy, watery eyes.</li>
 	<li><strong>Saline rinses:</strong> Rinsing the nose with salt water can wash out pollen and ease symptoms.</li>
 	<li><strong>Allergen avoidance:</strong> Simple steps such as wearing wraparound sunglasses, showering after being outdoors, and keeping windows shut on high-pollen days all help.</li>
 	<li><strong>Allergen immunotherapy:</strong> For severe, hard-to-control hay fever, a specialist may suggest immunotherapy (desensitisation). This treats the cause rather than masking symptoms, and is recommended for eligible patients in line with NICE guidance.</li>
</ul>
Combining two or three of these often controls symptoms that a single product cannot.
<h2>Conclusion</h2>
Is the hay fever injection effective? Yes, it helps reduce severe symptoms in some people temporarily. However, it is not a solution for all cases — the NHS discontinued its use due to safety concerns, it is not licensed for the treatment of hay fever in the UK, and it has effects on the entire body. In most cases, other solutions such as antihistamines, nasal sprays, and eye drops work well.

<h2>FAQs</h2>
<h3><strong>1. Can I still get the hay fever injection in the UK?</strong></h3>
It is harder to get than before. The main licensed product was discontinued in the UK in 2025, and it is not licensed for hay fever. Some private clinics may still offer an assessment, but a doctor must decide if it is appropriate.
<h3><strong>2. Is the hay fever jab available on the NHS?</strong></h3>
No. The NHS stopped offering it for hay fever in 2018 because it judged the risks too high for a seasonal allergy.
<h3><strong>3. Can I get the hay fever injection from my GP?</strong></h3>
NHS GPs do not provide it for hay fever. They will instead recommend antihistamines, nasal sprays, or, for severe cases, a referral to an allergy specialist.
<h3><strong>4. How quickly does the hay fever injection work?</strong></h3>
People who respond usually notice relief within 24 to 72 hours, as the steroid builds up in the body.
<h3><strong>5. Is the hay fever injection better than tablets?</strong></h3>
Not for most people. Tablets and sprays can be adjusted day-to-day and carry far fewer risks, whereas the injection cannot be reversed once given.
<h3><strong>6. What is the safest way to treat severe hay fever?</strong></h3>
Start with antihistamines and a steroid nasal spray early in the season, add eye drops if needed, and see a doctor if symptoms stay out of control. They can discuss specialist options such as immunotherapy.
